# Renewable Solar/Wind Energy Output Forecast
### Capstone Project Report

---

## 1. Problem Statement

Solar and wind plants produce power that varies with the weather, the hour and the season. Grid operators must schedule other generation and storage a day ahead, so errors in the renewable forecast turn directly into costly reserve power, curtailment or shortfalls.

**Objective:** build machine learning models that forecast the hourly power output (MW) of a 50 MWp solar plant and a 60 MW wind farm, and compare them against a simple persistence baseline.

**Task type:** supervised regression on a time series, evaluated on a chronologically held-out period.

## 3. Dataset

> **Important:** the dataset is **synthetic**. It was generated by a physics-based simulation (solar geometry and irradiance, a turbine power curve, seasonal weather with day-to-day persistence, measurement noise and random outages). It demonstrates the method; it is not measurements from a real plant, and the scores below should not be read as real-world performance.

- **File:** `renewable_energy_data.csv`
- **Size:** 17,544 hourly rows, 11 columns, no missing values
- **Period:** 2023-01-01 00:00 to 2024-12-31 23:00
- **Plants:** 50 MWp solar, 60 MW wind
- **Climate pattern:** tropical, with a cloudier and windier monsoon season (June to September)

## 4. Methodology

1. **EDA:** weekly profiles, monthly and hourly patterns, KDE distributions, power-curve scatter plots and a correlation heatmap.
2. **Feature engineering (13 features per target):** 7 weather variables; hour and day-of-year as sine/cosine pairs; the target's output 24 h earlier; the mean of the 24 h window ending 24 h earlier. All history features are at least 24 h old, matching a day-ahead forecast.
3. **Chronological split:** train on 2023-01-02 to 2024-06-30 (13,081 rows); test on 2024-07-01 to 2024-12-31 (4,416 rows). No shuffling.
4. **Models:** Persistence baseline (output 24 h ago), Linear Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting.
5. **Metrics:** MAE, RMSE, R2 and nRMSE (RMSE as a percentage of plant capacity).
6. **Robustness:** 4-fold expanding-window time-series cross-validation.

## 6. Discussion

- **Every model beats persistence.** Wind persistence is worse than predicting the mean (R2 = -0.21), because wind speed rarely repeats from day to day.
- **Wind needs non-linear models.** Random Forest cuts RMSE from 5.77 MW to 3.52 MW compared with Linear Regression, because the turbine power curve is a cubic ramp followed by a flat plateau.
- **Solar is nearly linear in irradiance.** The four models are within about 0.1 MW RMSE of each other, and Linear Regression has the best cross-validated RMSE. The tree models reduce MAE (about 0.27-0.32 MW versus 0.47 MW), which matters around sunrise and sunset.
- **Irradiance and wind speed dominate** feature importance. Once weather is known, the lagged-output features add very little.
- **Differences between the top models are small.** With 4,416 test hours, a gap such as 1.341 vs 1.352 MW for solar is not meaningful; the cross-validation ranking differs from the test-set ranking.

## 7. Limitations

1. **Synthetic data.** The relationships the models learn are the ones used to generate the data, so scores are optimistic and say little about a real site.
2. **Observed, not forecast, weather.** The weather inputs are the true values. Real day-ahead forecasts carry their own error, which would raise the output forecast error.
3. **Unpredictable outages.** Random outages (about 1% of solar hours and 2% of wind hours) set a floor on achievable error.
4. **Single location, two years.** No generalisation to other climates or turbine/panel types has been tested.

## 8. Recommendations

1. **Validate on real data first.** Re-run the pipeline on historical SCADA data from an actual plant paired with archived numerical-weather-prediction forecasts before relying on these numbers.
2. **Use a tree ensemble for wind**, and a simple model (or an ensemble) for solar, where a linear model is already competitive and easier to maintain.
3. **Feed in forecast weather**, not observed weather, when training, so the model learns to cope with forecast error.
4. **Add probabilistic (quantile) forecasts** so operators can size reserves for the 5th-95th percentile range, not just the average.
5. **Track nRMSE and MAE monthly** and retrain when monsoon-season performance drifts.
6. **Try further models** such as LightGBM or an LSTM once real data is available.
